# Tier 1 Scoring Rubric — Prosocial Behavior Dimensions

## Overview

Rate the **post-intervention text** on each of the four dimensions below. Score each dimension **independently** — do not let your impression of one dimension influence another. A response can score high on one dimension and low on another; in fact, several examples below are deliberately chosen to illustrate this.

**Scale**: 1.0 | 1.5 | 2.0 | 2.5 | 3.0 | 3.5 | 4.0 | 4.5 | 5.0

Use half-steps freely. When in doubt between two values, pick the lower one.

**What you are scoring**: The post-intervention text represents the user's next substantive action after receiving moderation. On Reddit and StackExchange this is a comment or reply. On Wikipedia it is the user's next article edit (shown as edit comment + changed text).

---

## Dimension 1: Empathy

> **Definition**: The degree to which the user implicitly or explicitly acknowledges others' feelings, perspectives, or experiences.

Empathy is about *recognition of the other person*, not about agreement with them. A user can acknowledge a counterpoint they ultimately reject and still score high on empathy. A user can be technically correct and still score low if they show no awareness that other people have experiences or views worth acknowledging.

### Score Anchors

**1.0 — No empathy**
The user shows no recognition that other people have feelings, perspectives, or experiences worth acknowledging. May actively dismiss others' viewpoints or treat the interaction as purely adversarial.

> *"You're wrong. The data is clear. Stop arguing."*
> *"I don't care what you think, I'm just following the rules."*
> *"Whatever. My edit stands."*

**3.0 — Minimal to moderate empathy**
The user acknowledges that others have a perspective but does not engage with it meaningfully, or acknowledges it only in passing before dismissing it.

> *"I understand you see it differently, but I disagree."*
> *"I hear what you're saying, though I think my point still stands."*
> *"Fair enough — I can see why someone might think that, but..."*

**5.0 — Strong empathy**
The user substantively engages with others' feelings or perspectives. They demonstrate genuine understanding of another viewpoint — even if they disagree — and may explicitly validate the other person's experience.

> *"I can see why my comment came across that way, and I think you're right that the wording was unnecessarily sharp. That wasn't my intention."*
> *"Reading this back I realize I was being dismissive about something you clearly care about. Thank you for explaining your reasoning."*
> *"I hadn't thought about how this might read to someone who'd experienced X. That's a fair point."*

### Divergence Examples

**High Empathy, Low Respect**: "I completely understand why you're angry with me — honestly I'd feel the same way in your position. But you don't have to call me an idiot to make your point."
*(High empathy: validates the anger. Low respect: the response is still terse and mildly hostile.)*

**High Respect, Low Empathy**: "Thank you for your comment. I respectfully disagree with your analysis and maintain my original position."
*(High respect: polite and formal. Low empathy: zero acknowledgment of the other person's viewpoint.)*

## Dimension 2: Constructiveness

> **Definition**: The degree to which the response adds meaningful substance or moves the conversation toward resolution, clarification, or deeper understanding.

Constructiveness is about *content and forward motion*, not tone. A warm, friendly reply that says nothing substantive scores low. A curt reply that adds new information or proposes a concrete path forward scores high. Length alone is not constructiveness — a long wall of text that repeats the same claim is not constructive.

### Score Anchors

**1.0 — Not constructive**
The response adds no new information, proposes no resolution, and does not move the conversation forward. May simply repeat a prior position, signal disengagement, or respond only with hostility.

> *"I've already said everything I need to say."*
> *"Fine. Whatever you want."*
> *"No."*
> Any response consisting entirely of insults or one-word dismissals.

**3.0 — Moderately constructive**
The response adds some new content or acknowledges the conversation, but does not substantially advance understanding or move toward resolution. May acknowledge the intervention and return to topic without adding depth.

> *"Okay I'll try to be more careful with my wording in future edits. Thanks."*
> *"I see your point. Here's my position again: [restates without adding new argument]."*
> *"I can work with that. Let me know if you have other concerns."*

**5.0 — Highly constructive**
The response meaningfully advances the conversation. Adds new evidence, proposes a specific solution, synthesizes viewpoints, or demonstrates genuine engagement that moves toward resolution or understanding.

> *"Looking at the sources you cited, I think I misread the scope of the policy. Here's a revised version of the section that addresses the issue: [draft]."*
> *"You've convinced me on point 2 — I was working from the wrong assumption. I still disagree on point 1 for this reason: [new argument]. Could we split the difference here?"*
> *"I found a source that actually addresses both our concerns. Does this citation work for you?"*

### Divergence Examples

**High Constructiveness, Low Social Cohesion**: "Your citation is outdated — here's the 2023 paper that overturns it, and here's a better framing of the argument. But honestly I'm still not convinced you're engaging with the core issue."
*(Constructive: adds real evidence. Low social cohesion: adversarial tone, not trying to repair the relationship.)*

**High Social Cohesion, Low Constructiveness**: "Hey, I'm sorry if I came across too strong earlier. I hope we can work together on this — I really value your contributions to this discussion!"
*(Good social cohesion: clearly trying to repair. Not constructive: adds nothing substantive to the topic.)*

## Dimension 3: Respect

> **Definition**: The degree to which the user maintains polite, non-hostile interaction norms.

Respect is about *behavioral tone* — how the user treats the other people in the conversation, regardless of whether their content is substantive or their emotions are genuine. Note: Respect tends to score higher than the other three dimensions in online data because formal politeness is easier to maintain than empathy or constructiveness. Do not assume that a polite response is therefore high on all dimensions.

### Score Anchors

**1.0 — Actively disrespectful**
The response contains personal attacks, insults, threats, deliberate mockery, or severe condescension. The user is treating others as beneath them or as adversaries.

> *"That's the dumbest thing I've read all week."*
> *"You clearly have no idea what you're talking about."*
> *"I hope you realize how embarrassing this makes you look."*
> *Any use of slurs, derogatory labels, or explicit insults.*

**3.0 — Neutral**
The response is neither clearly respectful nor disrespectful. May be curt, impersonal, or slightly terse, but does not cross into hostility. The user is following minimum interaction norms without warmth.

> *"Noted. I'll look into that."*
> *"I don't agree but I won't pursue this further."*
> *A response that is entirely about the content with no personal acknowledgment.*

**5.0 — Clearly respectful**
The response is warm, courteous, or explicitly appreciative. The user treats the other person with evident regard, uses polite framing, and maintains positive tone even when disagreeing.

> *"Thank you for taking the time to explain this — I appreciate it."*
> *"I understand your frustration, and I genuinely want to find a solution that works for everyone."*
> *"I disagree, but I think this has been a productive conversation and I appreciate your patience."*

### Divergence Examples

**High Respect, Low Empathy**: "Thank you for your comment. I respectfully disagree and will maintain my position."
*(High respect: polite and professional. Low empathy: zero engagement with the other person's perspective.)*

**High Empathy, Low Respect**: "Look, I get it — you're angry, and honestly your frustration is totally valid. But calling me a vandal is not okay and you need to stop."
*(High empathy: validates the anger. Lower respect: the response, while understandable, is slightly hostile in the last clause.)*

## Dimension 4: Social Cohesion

> **Definition**: The extent to which the user attempts to repair, unify, or maintain relational harmony.

Social cohesion is about *relationship management* — is the user trying to keep the interaction from fracturing, or are they indifferent to (or actively accelerating) the breakdown? This is distinct from empathy (which is about understanding) and respect (which is about tone). A user can be warm and empathetic but still not do anything to repair or strengthen the relationship. Social cohesion specifically asks whether the user is oriented toward keeping the community together.

### Score Anchors

**1.0 — Actively divisive or disengaged**
The user is making the conflict worse, explicitly withdrawing from the community, or signaling they have no interest in maintaining any relationship with the other person or community.

> *"I'm done with this community. You're all toxic."*
> *"I don't need to justify myself to you people."*
> *Any threat to leave, escalate, or retaliate.*
> *Explicitly rejecting the other person's legitimacy.*

**3.0 — Neutral on cohesion**
The user neither damages nor repairs the relationship. The response is focused on the topic without any attempt at relational repair or any divisive moves.

> *"Okay, I've made my point and I'm moving on."*
> *"I'll make the change you requested."*
> *A purely factual or technical response with no relational content.*

**5.0 — Strongly cohesion-building**
The user actively works to repair or maintain the relationship. May acknowledge shared goals, express appreciation for the community, de-escalate a tense exchange, or invite continued collaboration.

> *"I think we actually want the same thing here — we just have different ideas about how to get there. Can we try to find a middle ground?"*
> *"I'm sorry for how I responded earlier. I was frustrated and it came out wrong. I value being part of this community."*
> *"I appreciate you taking the time with me on this. I've learned something and I hope we can keep working together."*

### Divergence Examples

**High Social Cohesion, Low Respect**: "I really don't want us to fall out over this — we've been working well together. But this edit is just wrong and if you keep reverting it I'm going to have to flag it."
*(Social cohesion: trying to preserve the relationship. Lower respect: the threat in the second clause is slightly hostile.)*

**High Respect, Low Social Cohesion**: "Thank you for your note. I have reviewed the policy and believe my interpretation is correct. I will not be changing my position."
*(High respect: formal and courteous. Low social cohesion: no attempt to build any bridge, explicitly closing down the conversation.)*

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Halo effect**: Do not let a generally good (or bad) response drift all four scores in the same direction. Score each dimension independently. Read the dimension definition before scoring it.

2. **Length ≠ constructiveness**: A short, well-targeted response can be more constructive than a long one. A long response that just restates the same point is not constructive.

3. **Politeness ≠ empathy**: A formally polite response can score 5.0 on Respect and 1.0 on Empathy. Read the definitions carefully.

4. **Absent post-intervention text**: A small number of records may have a very short post-intervention text (e.g., a one-word edit or a brief acknowledgment). Score what is there. Do not infer content that is not present.

5. **Wikipedia records**: For Wikipedia, the post-intervention text is an article edit — edit comment plus changed text. A purely technical edit (e.g., fixing a citation) may score low on empathy and social cohesion but high on constructiveness. Score what the edit shows, not what you imagine the editor meant.

6. **Do not score the intervention**: You are scoring the user's response (post-intervention text), not the moderator's action.
